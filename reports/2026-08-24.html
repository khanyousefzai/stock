How to read the ratios

Colour bands apply to every table and card below

Five ratios drive everything in this report. Each is colour-coded the same way wherever it appears: jade is healthy, amber is acceptable but worth watching, red is a flag that needs a reason.

P/E

Price to Earnings

What you pay for each dollar the company earns. A P/E of 20 means twenty years of current earnings to pay back the share price. Low can mean cheap — or that earnings are expected to fall.

below 20cheap
20 – 50premium
above 50expensive

P/B

Price to Book

Share price against the accounting value of the company's net assets. Useful for banks and miners; misleading for asset-light firms and any company that has bought back a lot of stock, which shrinks book equity toward zero.

below 3asset-backed
3 – 15typical
above 15thin

ROE

Return on Equity

Profit generated per dollar of shareholder capital. The single best quick measure of business quality — but check debt alongside it, because leverage inflates ROE without improving the business.

above 30%excellent
10 – 30%solid
below 10%weak

D/E

Debt to Equity

Borrowings against shareholder capital. Central to Shariah screening, though note the AAOIFI test measures debt against market capitalisation with a 33% ceiling — not against book equity as shown here.

below 0.5low
0.5 – 1.5moderate
above 1.5leveraged

Curr

Current Ratio

Short-term assets against short-term bills. Below 1.0 means the company owes more within a year than it holds in liquid assets — survivable for a cash-generative giant, dangerous for anyone else.

above 2.0strong
1.0 – 2.0adequate
below 1.0tight

The 105-point score

Every one of the 48 stocks is scored identically. Six components are graded on a sliding scale — there are no arbitrary cut-offs inside a component — and summed. The weighting deliberately puts more on the balance sheet (D/E plus current ratio = 35 points) than on the analyst target (26 points), because forecasts are opinions and the balance sheet is a fact.

Shariah compliance notes

The screens genuinely disagree, and this run proves it. Checking the issuers' own holdings files this morning: SPUS holds 34 of the 35 US names; HLAL holds 32. Oracle and Home Depot are in SPUS but absent from HLAL — FTSE measures debt against total assets while S&P measures against market cap, and both names fail the stricter test. Thirty-two names clear both.

ASML is in neither, and that is a domicile exclusion rather than a compliance failure: both funds track US-only universes, so a Netherlands issuer cannot appear regardless of how it screens. Nothing has vetted it for you here.

Debt is screened against market capitalisation under the AAOIFI standard, with a 33% ceiling — not against book equity. So a high book D/E in section C is not automatically disqualifying, which is why Apple (0.78) and Home Depot (3.77) can both sit inside SPUS. Zoya is looser than either index screen; a stock passing Zoya has not necessarily passed S&P or FTSE.

Purification. Even compliant companies earn some incidental interest income. The standard practice is to calculate the non-compliant portion of dividends received and donate it. The fund providers publish annual purification ratios; for direct stock holdings you calculate it yourself.

Excluded as non-compliant and deliberately absent from this universe: MCD, CMG, DE, HON, GE, LMT, RTX, TMUS, DIS, AMGN. Questionable — screens disagree, decide for yourself: COST, WMT, NFLX, SBUX, INTU, CAT.
